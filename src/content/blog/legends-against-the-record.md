Every region has its buried treasure stories. Upper Austria has hundreds: the chest in the
castle ruin, the abbey gold walled in by blindfolded masons, the three tubs buried in the wood
"in wartime, a hundred years ago".

The question I wanted answered is a dry one. **Do these stories sit on anything?** Not "where
is the gold", but: if you put the legends, the old newspaper reports of actual finds and the
official registers of known sites on one map, do they line up more often than chance, and
what happens to a good story when you check it?

This is desk research with agents. Nobody dug anywhere, and further down is the paragraph
of law that says why nobody will.

## Three kinds of evidence, kept apart

The map has three kinds of pins, and the whole point is that they never get mixed:

| kind | what it is | where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **documented** | a site or find an authority has on record | open registers: the heritage office's lists, the province's history service (castles, vanished seats, earthworks, Roman sites) |
| **reported** | a find somebody wrote about at the time | digitised newspapers, 1850 to 1938 |
| **told** | a legend | printed collections, mostly one from 1932 |

After two rounds it holds about 5,500 documented pins, 126 newspaper reports and 233 legends.
Later layers add events (battles, fires, floods), the places where money moved (tolls, post
stations, ferries, mints), Roman roads, and about a thousand place, farm and house names,
most of them recorded around 1830, that remember something: *Maut*, *Burgstall*, *Urfahr*.

A fourth view, which I call the puzzle, lists only the places where a story or a report lies
close to a documented site of a fitting kind. A treasure legend at a ruin that is in the
register. A coin report next to a Roman road.

## What the layers are worth

It would be easy to make this look better than it is, so here are the limits as they stand:

- **Newspaper text is machine-read Fraktur.** It is good enough to find an article and bad
  at exactly the things that matter: names and numbers. Of 138 reports, 64 rest on the search
  snippet only. Every figure that counts has to be read on the page image.
- **Legends are placed by a reader.** About a hundred sit at the main village of their
  municipality because the story says no more than that.
- **Most legends are stock motifs.** Of the 233, only 29 name a time at all. Thirteen are
  hoards buried in a war.
- **The roads are conjecture.** All 24 Roman road segments in the source are rated that way.
- **A name is a memory, not a find.** Farms called *Schatz* or *Gold* usually had an owner
  by that name.
- The rules for what counts as a "fitting" site are my first guess and were tuned against
  nothing.

So the map does not prove anything. It is a tool for choosing which story is worth an
evening.

## The best story, checked

The one I liked most: an abbey's treasure, walled up by masons who were led in blindfolded.
The prelate who knew the place died. In the version I first read, the wall was in a house in
the provincial capital, and a newspaper of 1931 reports a jug of sixteen gold coins found
inside a wall in the old town there. A legend and a find in the same town.

It took one night to take apart.

1. **The first print says something else.** The story was first printed in 1864. There, the
   wall is in the abbey. The capital only appears in a retelling of 1967. The match with the
   1931 jug rested on a rewording a hundred years younger than the story.
2. **The parish register settles the death.** The retelling has the prelate die in the
   capital. His death entry of 1803 is in the scanned parish register. He died at the abbey.
3. **The only clue left is a sound.** One mason is said to have heard a clock going above
   him. No abbot of the period fits the whole story.

My estimate afterwards: about 85 % that nothing was ever lost, 10 % that something was hidden
from the state and quietly spent a few years later, a few percent that it is still in the
abbey. The house in the capital: zero.

## A legend that does sit on a find

A ruin in the Salzkammergut has the standard chest-of-gold tale. In July 1881 a local paper
reported that two sedan-chair carriers had found 36 Roman coins there, close under the
surface. A second paper, four weeks later, has a conservator describing some of them, from
Trajan to a son of Constantine, and supposes a Roman fort under the castle. (That second
report I have only as machine-read text so far, not checked on the page.) The heritage
register lists Iron Age and Roman finds for the site.

So this legend is next to a real find. But the find was made 145 years ago, it was common
coinage, the site is a protected monument, and I do not yet know whether the tale is older
than the find or grew out of it. That order is the whole question, and it is still open.

## Where real hoards came from

Reading all the reports against all the legends, one pattern was hard to miss. **The hoards
that really turned up did not come out of castles and abbeys.** They came out of ordinary
houses and farmyards, by accident.

One example, ten kilometres from Linz: in October 1926 a pot came to light in a farmyard, 25
to 30 centimetres down, and broke. Seven gold ducats and 65 silver thalers, the latest coin
from 1625. In December the coins were still with the finder. Where they are today, I have not
found written down anywhere.

That is what a real one looks like: no story attached, a plain date, a depth, a coin list.

## What the method learned

- **Go to the first print before anything else.** Later collections shorten a tale and move
  it. The 1932 collection even marks the tales its editor only knew from print.
- **A legend near a find proves nothing until you know which came first.**
- **The second newspaper report is the good one.** The first says "coins were found". The
  one that follows a few weeks later often has the list. Search six weeks past the first.
- **Two readers for every handwritten source**, and a crop for every number. How that works
  is in [Eight models read a letter from 1726](/blog/old-handwriting-model-test/).
- **A bot check is a stop sign.** Two of the digital libraries answer scripted requests with
  a challenge. The agent does not work around it. I open those pages myself, or we find
  another copy of the same volume.
- **Negative results stay on the map.** The abbey pin now carries a note: what the check
  found. A story that has been tested is worth more than one that has not, whatever the
  answer was.

## The law, which decides the scope

In Austria this is federal law, the Monument Protection Act (*Denkmalschutzgesetz*), in the
version in force since September 2024. It was checked in the federal legal database on the
day the project started. In short:

- **Searching on the spot needs a permit** from the federal heritage office: digging, on land
  or under water, metal detectors, systematic measurement (§ 10).
- **The permit is only given if a person with a relevant university degree leads the work.**
  A hobbyist cannot hold it alone.
- **Chance finds must be reported without delay**, and the spot left as it is for five
  working days (§§ 8 and 9).
- **Searching without a permit** costs up to 25,400 euros, and the finds can be forfeited
  (§ 37).
- The civil code gives finder and landowner half each (§ 399 ABGB), and takes the finder's
  half away if the search was unlawful (§ 400).

Reading archives, newspapers, old maps and published laser terrain models at a desk is none
of that. So that is where this project stays. If a case ever ends with a place worth
looking at, the route is a dossier to the heritage office or to an archaeologist who can
apply, not a weekend with a detector.

*This is my reading of the statute, not legal advice, and it covers Austria only.*

## Why the map is not public

Most of what is on it comes from open data. I still keep it behind a login. A map that puts
legends next to find spots is an invitation, and publishing the exact place of an unrecorded
site is how sites get emptied. The same rule as in my
[shipwreck project](/projects/wreckscan/): the method is open, positions are not.

If you need access to the map, for research or because you work on one of these places, ask
me by e-mail: [info@joseftot.com](mailto:info@joseftot.com).

What I can say in public is the result so far. The stories are mostly stories. The finds are
mostly accidents. And the interesting work is in the small set of places where a dated
report, a register entry and the lie of the land point the same way.
